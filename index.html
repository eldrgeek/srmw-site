The book

A novel about writing the novel it is.

It was written during NaNoWriMo in 2011 and has been quietly finished ever since. The author is a character in it. So is his inner critic, who never stops. So is God, who is a woman, drinks, swears, and is markedly smarter than the author. So is his dead mother, who shows up to give writing advice on the grounds that characters live as long as someone remembers them.

Also present: the Not Quite Omniscient Narrator, an Adversary, an antimuse, the heat death of the universe, and the metanovel itself — which becomes self-aware partway through and starts negotiating.

“I could be a better book, but I would be happy to be the book that I am — if I were finished. Please stop editing me. Let me be finished!” The metanovel, printed page 5

He was on his fifty-fifth draft when the book said that.

The original edition

Buy the broken one.

The Kindle edition went up on 27 February 2025 with the typos in it. And the dead links. And a publisher's disclaimer that opens:

“This book may contains certain characters who certain people may believe may have a certain resemblance to certain deities…” Publisher's Disclaimer, printed page 7

That is not an oversight being dressed up as charm. It is the book winning an argument. It asked to stop being edited on page 5, and it got its way. Everything unfixed after that is evidence.

Two hundred pages later, the inner critic pays the author his one and only compliment, immediately regrets it, and tells him to finish. The reply, printed exactly as it appears on page 213:

“OK,” I said. “Just two more page.” Printed page 213 — one letter short, since 2012

Nobody caught it. Nobody was going to. It sits there in the last conversation the book ever has about being unfinished.

A corrected edition is coming. It is being written with the SOMA team. It will be cleaner, and something will be missing from it. There is no ship date, and inventing one would be the exact procrastination this book is about — so there isn't one here.

Exhibit

Two AI hosts read the book. Then they skipped two words.

In November 2024, a jailbroken NotebookLM deep-dive tore through the whole metanovel with real enthusiasm and a great deal of profanity. Around minute ten, the hosts begin discussing the author's dead child.

There is no dead child. The sentence they were reading is in Chapter 29:

“He started to panic. It was starting to get dark. And cold. He imagined the two kids lost somewhere in the dense woods.” Chapter 29 — the sentence they are working from

The word lost is genuinely on the page. So are the two words that govern it. They kept one and dropped the other — and then repeated the mistake three and a half minutes later, by which point it had been promoted to grief, guilt, and anger.

This is not a hallucination. Nothing was invented. It is inattention — the failure a hurried human reader makes on a train. Which is worth saying out loud, because “the AI made it up” is the diagnosis everyone reaches for, and it is usually the wrong one.

The chapter they misread is the book's demonstration of the Third Classic Blunder: never confuse what you imagined with what happened. They read a chapter about mistaking the imagined for the real, and mistook the imagined for the real.

So the recording has been interrupted. It stops itself at each point the error is spoken, shows the text, and resumes.

SRMW: The Movie

A film of the book, made by AI actors, voices, and sets — about a book that accidentally predicted its own production method. Five drafts as five acts, each a coherent unit, releasable on its own.

There are three recursion layers: the book; the film of the book; and the making of the film, in which the AI cast hits every blocker the author hit and uses the book's own techniques to get unstuck. The two AI podcast hosts from the 2024 recording are the Greek chorus.

There is no trailer here, on purpose. A polished sizzle reel would contradict the premise. This is the actual state of the production:

The last thing in the book

There's always room.

Near the end, the author stops selling. He describes a place he built on the worst day of his life — which was about thirty minutes in a parking lot in New Hampshire during which, in the end, nothing happened at all. God, who is a character he invented and who is smarter than he is, gave him a deadline: “Make one. Now!”

So he made one, wrote down the invitation, and left it near the back of a book almost nobody has read:

“I'd like to invite you, the reader reading this, to visit my safe place whenever you like. There's always room. And it's easy to get there. Just believe you can; decide to go there; and imagine that you're there. If at first you don't succeed, go meta.” Printed page 214

The inner critic has notes. The inner critic always has notes.

The offer is still open, it costs nothing, and the man who made it is currently letting a crew of machines finish his movie — which is either the strangest possible endorsement of the technique or the only one.
