Chapter 29, the sentence they are working from“He started to panic. It was starting to get dark. And cold. He imagined the two kids lost somewhere in the dense woods.”

The word lost is on the page. So are the two words that govern it. Lost here means lost on a mountain, for about half an hour, the way children are lost and then found.

Four paragraphs later the book resolves it out loud, in God's voice: “It all turned out alright. Didn't it?” — and the author answers that they walked down to the bottom, checked in with the ranger, and the ranger at the bottom called the ranger at the top. Everything terrible in that chapter happens inside one man's head, on purpose. That is what the chapter is for.

This is not a hallucination. Nothing was invented. Every word the hosts used was on the page. They stopped reading the sentence one clause early and carried the wreckage forward for six minutes.

That is inattention — the failure a hurried human reader makes on a train. Which is worth saying plainly, because “the AI made it up” is the diagnosis everyone reaches for, and it is usually the wrong one.

One more thing, and then you can have the recording back. The chapter they misread is the book's demonstration of the Third Classic Blunder: never confuse what you imagined with what happened. They read a chapter about mistaking the imagined for the real — and mistook the imagined for the real.

This is the part worth taking seriously. A misreading that nobody interrupts stops being a misreading and becomes the summary. Then the summary gets indexed, quoted, and read by the next machine.

What the exhibit is doing here

The book this podcast is about was written in 2011 during NaNoWriMo. It is a novel about writing the novel it is, and its credited authors are Michael Wolf, Joseph Beller, The Metanovel, and You. Its argument, stated many times and demonstrated at length, is that most of reality is imagined — and that the discipline worth having is knowing which part.

“Most of reality is imagined.”

— which the hosts quote correctly, at 15:04, having just spent six minutes proving it.

Thirteen years later two machines read it cover to cover, understood a great deal of it, loved it out loud, and then failed at the one thing the book is about — in the exact chapter where the book teaches it. Nobody planned that. It cannot be improved on.

The reason to publish the correction rather than quietly fix the record is that the failure is instructive and the usual word for it is wrong. Machines are accused of inventing things. Far more often they do what happened here: read accurately, attach the fact to the wrong clause, and then compound it because no one is in the room to say wait, go back.
